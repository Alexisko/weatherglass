How Weatherglass computes its numbers

Methodology version 0.2 · October 2026

Every number on Weatherglass is computed by the site's own code from published climate data, using definitions from the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the international climate-extremes community (ETCCDI). This page explains what the data is, how each figure is defined, and where the numbers can be wrong.

The data

Weatherglass uses ERA5, the global climate reanalysis produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) for the Copernicus Climate Change Service. A reanalysis is a reconstruction of past weather: a weather model is run over the past and continuously corrected with millions of observations from weather stations, balloons, ships, aircraft and satellites. The result is a complete, consistent record for every place on Earth, hour by hour, since 1940.

The data is downloaded from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, one request per place, and cached in your browser. Daily values (highest and lowest temperature, precipitation, snowfall, sunshine and daylight) follow the place's local time.

Earlier versions of this site used a single climate-model simulation. A climate model's "2013–2022" is a plausible decade, not the weather that actually happened, so it has been replaced by ERA5.

The reference period: 1991–2020

A climate normal is an average over 30 years, long enough to smooth out year-to-year swings. Weatherglass uses 1991–2020, the current WMO standard reference period (WMO-No. 1203). A period that crosses the new year, such as December to February, occurs 29 complete times in that window.

Data completeness follows WMO practice. A month counts towards an average only if no more than 5 days are missing, with no more than 3 in a row. Totals and counts need every day. A normal is published only if at least 80 % of the years (24 of 30) are usable. ERA5 is complete, so these rules rarely apply, but they are built in.

Definitions

FigureDefinition
Daytime highAverage of the daily maximum air temperature at 2 m. For a month: the average of the 30 monthly means. For a longer period, each month is weighted by its number of days.
Night lowAverage of the daily minimum air temperature at 2 m. The minimum usually happens around sunrise.
Most days / most nightsThe range between the 10th and 90th percentile of all daily values in the period over the 30 years. 8 days in 10 fall inside it. Percentiles use linear interpolation (the method used by spreadsheets, R and NumPy).
Day with rainA day with at least 1.0 mm of precipitation (WMO / ETCCDI "R1mm"). Smaller amounts are mostly drizzle that models produce too often. Where snow is common, the site says "rain or snow", because precipitation includes melted snow.
Share of daysDays with rain divided by all days in the period, over the 30 years.
PrecipitationTotal rain and melted snow (water equivalent), averaged over the 30 years: per month, or for the whole selected period.
Day with snowA day with at least 1 cm of fresh snowfall.
SunshineHours a day with strong direct sunlight (direct irradiance above 120 W/m², the WMO definition), as modelled. Shown with the share of daylight it represents.
DaylightHours between sunrise and sunset.

Known limits

  • A grid cell, not a weather station. ERA5 cells are about 25–30 km wide. Coasts, valleys, mountains and city heat islands are smoothed, so local extremes are muted. Open-Meteo adjusts temperature to the elevation of the exact point; other variables are not adjusted.
  • Precipitation is less certain than temperature, especially for thunderstorms, mountains and the tropics.
  • Sunshine is modelled from cloud and radiation fields. It is not measured by a sunshine recorder.
  • Differences from official normals are expected. National weather services use station data and sometimes other day boundaries (for example 09:00 to 09:00).
  • The climate is changing. 1991–2020 normals describe the recent past. Recent years in many places are warmer than these normals.

How the numbers are checked

  • Every statistic is computed by small, unit-tested functions. The tests run on every change.
  • Planned: a published comparison against official normals for about ten reference stations in different climates.
  • Changes to definitions are versioned. This page shows the version in use.
